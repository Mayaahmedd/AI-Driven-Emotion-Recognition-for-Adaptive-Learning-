\chapter{Background}\label{chap:background-foundations}

\section{Emotion Recognition in Learning}

Emotion recognition means inferring a learner's affective state from observable behaviour, such as patterns in the face captured from a webcam \cite{lek_academic_2023,florestiyanto_emotion_2024}. \emph{Facial Expression Recognition} (FER) is the computer-vision task of mapping face images or short face sequences to emotion-related categories automatically \cite{lek_academic_2023}. In learning environments, FER is attractive because it can supply repeated, moment-by-moment estimates while the learner continues studying, rather than interrupting the session for self-report questionnaires \cite{vistorte_integrating_2024}. Compared with multimodal or physiological sensing, webcam-based FER uses hardware already common in online courses and places a comparatively low extra burden on learners and institutions \cite{vistorte_integrating_2024,florestiyanto_emotion_2024,lek_academic_2023}.

Educational research usually targets \emph{academic emotions} rather than the label sets common in general-purpose FER benchmarks \cite{pekrun_control-value_2006,dmello_language_2012,lek_academic_2023}. Classical FER datasets such as FER-2013 assign static face photographs to basic categories, such as anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, and neutral, that were designed for general expression recognition, not for study sessions \cite{lek_academic_2023}. Academic emotions, by contrast, are feelings tied to learning activities and task appraisals; in tutoring research they include engagement, confusion, frustration, and boredom \cite{dmello_language_2012,pekrun_control-value_2006}. These states are pedagogically meaningful because they are linked to attention, effort, persistence, and how information is processed during study \cite{pekrun_control-value_2006,tyng_influences_2017}. Educational FER therefore shifts from basic-emotion image taxonomies toward four-state academic benchmarks, of which DAiSEE is the standard public reference \cite{gupta_daisee_2016,lek_academic_2023,florestiyanto_emotion_2024}.

The \emph{DAiSEE} dataset (\emph{D}ataset for \emph{A}ffective \emph{S}tates in \emph{E}-\emph{E}nvironments) is a public collection of short learner webcam \emph{video} clips, each annotated for engagement, boredom, confusion, and frustration \cite{gupta_daisee_2016}. A \emph{dataset}, in machine learning, is a structured set of labelled examples used to fit and evaluate models \cite{lek_academic_2023}. DAiSEE is widely used because it records spontaneous study-like behaviour over time, whereas much of the broader FER literature still trains on static face \emph{photographs} from general-purpose image sets; those collections are useful for benchmarking detectors but capture little of how academic affect evolves during an ongoing lesson \cite{lek_academic_2023,florestiyanto_emotion_2024}. The methodology chapter describes how DAiSEE-style labels are used as the emotion targets for the FER module in this work \cite{gupta_daisee_2016}.

\section{Deep Learning Components Used in This Thesis}

In traditional machine learning for FER, a human engineer typically designs the input representation: the model does not learn the face end-to-end from raw pixels. Early systems often combined hand-crafted descriptors, such as Local Binary Patterns (LBP), Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG), or facial landmarks, with a separate classifier \cite{lek_academic_2023,rao_recognition_2021}. Deep learning, by contrast, is a machine learning approach in which a model learns many layers of representations directly from data \cite{aly_revolutionizing_2025}. A neural network is the model structure used in deep learning; it maps input values to output predictions through connected layers \cite{gupta_edfa_2023}. In FER, deep learning is attractive because it can learn complex facial patterns, from edges and local regions to higher-level expression cues, that are difficult to specify as manual rules \cite{lek_academic_2023}. Three broad modelling families used in the field are compared in Table~\ref{tab:fer_model_families}.

\subsection{FER Model Families}\label{sec:bg:fer_families}

Educational FER systems can be grouped into three families at a high level. Traditional pipelines rely on engineered features and a separate decision module. CNN-based models learn hierarchical spatial features automatically from images. Transformer-based models apply self-attention to image patches or tokens so that relations between facial regions can be modelled explicitly \cite{lek_academic_2023,su_leveraging_2024,liu_dynamic_nodate,aly_revolutionizing_2025}. The comparison is conceptual: real systems may combine elements from more than one family, for example a CNN backbone followed by a temporal transformer head.

CNN-based models remain the dominant family in educational FER because convolutional filters learn local structure, such as edges, eyes, mouth shape, wrinkles, and muscle-related patterns, before combining them into higher-level representations \cite{lek_academic_2023,rao_recognition_2021,aly_revolutionizing_2025}. Transformer-based approaches are an emerging alternative: they can learn which regions, such as the eyes, mouth, eyebrows, and cheeks, should influence one another when forming an expression estimate \cite{su_leveraging_2024,liu_dynamic_nodate}. The appropriate family depends on dataset size, label quality, and available compute rather than on any single architecture name.

A \emph{Convolutional Neural Network} (CNN) is a neural network designed for images \cite{aly_revolutionizing_2025}. It applies convolution filters across the input so that local patterns, including edges, textures, and parts of the face, are detected and stacked into deeper features \cite{gupta_edfa_2023,aly_revolutionizing_2025}. In FER, CNNs often employ deep backbones such as ResNet, VGG, or EfficientNet as spatial feature extractors before classification or temporal modelling \cite{he_resnet_2016,asaju_adaptive_2022,komaravalli_detecting_2023,shiri_detection_2024}.

A \emph{feature extractor} is the part of a model that converts raw images into compact numeric representations called features \cite{komaravalli_detecting_2023}. A \emph{backbone} is the main feature-extractor network used before the final prediction layers \cite{aly_revolutionizing_2025}. In video FER, the backbone typically processes each frame independently; a separate temporal module may then aggregate frame-level features across a clip \cite{asaju_affects_2021,asaju_adaptive_2022}. The methodology chapter describes the specific backbone selected for this work.

Transfer learning means starting from a model that was pre-trained on a large general-purpose dataset, then fine-tuning it for the target FER task \cite{komaravalli_detecting_2023}. \emph{Pre-trained} means the network has already learned generic visual patterns, such as edges and object parts, before task-specific training on an educational dataset \cite{aly_revolutionizing_2025}. This approach is widely used because educational FER datasets are typically much smaller than general image collections such as ImageNet \cite{lek_academic_2023}.

Beyond standard CNN backbones, some FER studies adopt other families. Xception and EfficientNet are CNN architectures designed to improve feature quality and computational efficiency \cite{komaravalli_detecting_2023,shiri_detection_2024}. A \emph{Vision Transformer} (ViT) applies transformer self-attention to image patches instead of convolution filters alone \cite{tulsani_realtime_2025,su_leveraging_2024}. Through attention, such models can relate distant face regions, for example the eyes and mouth or the eyebrows and cheeks, when estimating an expression \cite{su_leveraging_2024,liu_dynamic_nodate}. A \emph{transformer} more generally is a sequence model that uses self-attention to model dependencies between elements in parallel \cite{su_leveraging_2024,liu_dynamic_nodate}.

\emph{InceptionNet} is another CNN family that uses multi-branch convolution blocks with different kernel sizes in parallel \cite{gupta_daisee_2016}. It is often used as a benchmark backbone in FER studies on academic-affect data \cite{gupta_daisee_2016}.

Classical components also appear in hybrid FER pipelines. A \emph{Support Vector Machine} (SVM) is a classifier that separates classes by finding a maximum-margin boundary in feature space \cite{rao_recognition_2021,turkes_enhanced_2025}. \emph{PCA} and \emph{SVD} are dimensionality-reduction methods that compress features while retaining major information patterns \cite{santoni_convolutional_2023}. \emph{SMOTE} is an oversampling method that creates synthetic minority examples to reduce class imbalance \cite{santoni_convolutional_2023}.

Some models use specialised feature terms. A \emph{GCN}, or Graph Convolutional Network, learns from graph-structured inputs such as landmark graphs \cite{rianto_engagement_2025}. \emph{Action Units} are localised facial muscle movement codes used in facial analysis \cite{santoni_convolutional_2023,rianto_engagement_2025}. \emph{LDAM} is an imbalance-aware loss that increases class margins for better minority-class separation \cite{rianto_engagement_2025}.

\section{Temporal Modeling in Video}\label{sec:bg:temporal}

Temporal modelling means learning how information changes over time, not only what appears in a single frame \cite{asaju_adaptive_2022,mehta_three-dimensional_2022}. In educational FER, many academic states build gradually, so a short frame sequence often provides stronger evidence than one still image \cite{abedi_improving_2021}. Academic affect also tends to evolve over seconds rather than appearing fully in one snapshot; models trained only on static images may therefore underrepresent how emotions fluctuate during live study \cite{lek_academic_2023}. Video-based FER consequently operates on sampled frame sequences rather than isolated photographs \cite{asaju_adaptive_2022,rao_recognition_2021}.

\emph{Frame sampling} means selecting a fixed number of frames from each video clip for processing \cite{abedi_improving_2021}. Giving every training example the same temporal length makes batching and optimisation more stable, because each input has the same sequence shape \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022}. A common lightweight strategy is to use on the order of tens of frames per clip rather than processing dense video at hundreds of frames per second \cite{asaju_adaptive_2022,abedi_improving_2021}. Sparse, fixed-length sampling keeps computation manageable for training and deployment while still capturing how expression evolves over a short window \cite{lek_academic_2023,rao_recognition_2021}. The trade-off is that very brief or subtle expression changes between sampled frames may be missed, which is why sampling policy and clip length remain design choices in the literature \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022}.

Deep learning pipelines for video FER increasingly combine architectures suited to different signal types. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are effective at extracting spatial features from individual face images, such as edges, eyes, mouth shape, wrinkles, and local muscle patterns, and are well suited to appearance at one time step \cite{aly_revolutionizing_2025,rao_recognition_2021}. Frame-independent CNNs alone, however, do not model how expression changes across time. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), especially Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, are designed to capture temporal dependencies in sequences and are widely used when the task requires analysing how emotional evidence accumulates or shifts over a clip \cite{hochreiter_lstm_1997,leong_deep_2020}. Applied directly to raw pixels, RNN-based models can struggle with spatial feature extraction and may suffer from vanishing-gradient effects on very long sequences \cite{hochreiter_lstm_1997}. A common hybrid strategy therefore encodes each sampled frame with a CNN or other spatial backbone into a feature vector, then applies an RNN or bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) over the resulting sequence \cite{gupta_daisee_2016,asaju_adaptive_2022}. Long-term Recurrent Convolutional Network (LRCN) architectures follow this pattern explicitly \cite{gupta_daisee_2016}. Educational FER studies on datasets such as DAiSEE have adopted similar CNN-plus-recurrent designs because academic emotions often build gradually over short video windows \cite{rao_recognition_2021,asaju_adaptive_2022,mehta_three-dimensional_2022,leong_deep_2020,gupta_daisee_2016}.

An \emph{LSTM} (Long Short-Term Memory) is a recurrent network that stores useful past information while processing a sequence step by step \cite{hochreiter_lstm_1997,leong_deep_2020}. A \emph{BiLSTM} (bidirectional LSTM) reads the sequence in both forward and backward directions, so each time step can use past and future context within the clip \cite{asaju_adaptive_2022,tulsani_realtime_2025}. In hybrid video FER pipelines, BiLSTM layers are often placed after per-frame CNN features to encode temporal emotion patterns over a clip \cite{asaju_adaptive_2022,leong_deep_2020}.

A \emph{3D CNN} applies convolutions over height, width, and time together, so spatial and temporal patterns are learned in one operation \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022}. \emph{C3D} is an early 3D CNN design used for short video clips \cite{gupta_daisee_2016}. Such models can be effective on short educational clips but are often heavier than a two-stage frame-wise CNN followed by an RNN when only a sparse subset of frames is used \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022,abedi_improving_2021}. \emph{LRCN} (Long-term Recurrent Convolutional Network) names the explicit hybrid pattern in which CNN frame features feed a recurrent temporal module \cite{gupta_daisee_2016}; it serves as an alternative formulation to naming the CNN and RNN stages separately. The methodology chapter describes the specific temporal design adopted in this work.

\section{Attention Mechanisms}

An \emph{attention mechanism} is a learnable weighting procedure that assigns higher influence to the parts of an input that are most informative for the current prediction \cite{vaswani_attention_2017,mehta_three-dimensional_2022,su_leveraging_2024}. Instead of treating every feature or time step equally, the model learns where to focus. In FER, attention is widely used because webcam clips contain redundant frames, noisy backgrounds, and weak or misleading facial cues; reweighting features or time steps often improves robustness compared with simple averaging \cite{liu_dynamic_nodate,aly_revolutionizing_2025,lek_academic_2023}. The following subsections summarise three common placements in modern pipelines: channel and spatial attention within CNN features, temporal attention over encoded frame sequences, and self-attention in transformer-style models that relate facial regions to one another.

\subsection{Channel and Spatial Attention in CNN Features}\label{sec:bg:channel_attention}

\emph{Channel attention} recalibrates the feature maps produced by a CNN backbone by learning importance weights over channels \cite{hu_squeeze_2018,aly_revolutionizing_2025}. A \emph{squeeze-and-excitation} (SE) block is a widely used form: it pools spatial information within each channel, passes a compact summary through a small network, and rescales channels so that cues associated with brows, eyes, or mouth regions can be emphasised before features are passed to a temporal module \cite{hu_squeeze_2018,mehta_three-dimensional_2022}. A \emph{channel} here means one dimension of the tensor representation at a given layer, not a single emotion label \cite{liu_dynamic_nodate}. \emph{Spatial attention}, by contrast, learns which locations within a feature map matter most for the current face \cite{aly_revolutionizing_2025}. The Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) combines channel and spatial attention in one block and is sometimes used as an alternative to SE-style recalibration in FER backbones \cite{aly_revolutionizing_2025,mehta_three-dimensional_2022}.

\subsection{Temporal Attention}\label{sec:bg:temporal_attention}

\emph{Temporal attention} applies learnable weights over the time steps of an encoded clip \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022,su_leveraging_2024}. After sparse frame sampling, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:bg:temporal}, not every frame carries equal affective evidence: neutral or repetitive frames may dominate a simple mean or last-hidden-state summary, whereas brief moments of clear confusion or frustration can be more diagnostic \cite{liu_dynamic_nodate,abedi_improving_2021}. Temporal attention learns to up-weight informative time steps and down-weight less informative ones when forming a clip-level representation \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022,tulsani_realtime_2025}. This complements fixed-length sampling rather than replacing it: sampling chooses \emph{which} frames enter the model, while temporal attention decides \emph{how much} each encoded frame should contribute to the final decision. Transformer-style temporal modules, discussed below, extend the same idea with self-attention over the frame sequence \cite{su_leveraging_2024,liu_dynamic_nodate}.

\subsection{Self-Attention and Related Architectures}\label{sec:bg:self_attention}

\emph{Self-attention} lets each element in a sequence attend to every other element, so dependencies are modelled in parallel rather than only step by step \cite{vaswani_attention_2017,su_leveraging_2024}. In face-centred models, this can learn relations between eyes and mouth, between eyebrows and cheeks, and broader global expression patterns: the network learns facial structure and also learns which regions should influence one another \cite{su_leveraging_2024,liu_dynamic_nodate}. A \emph{Vision Transformer} (ViT) applies self-attention to fixed image patches, treating spatial layout as a token sequence \cite{tulsani_realtime_2025,su_leveraging_2024}. Educational and dynamic FER work has also proposed specialised temporal transformers; for example, a \emph{Differential Temporal Transformer} (DTFormer) is designed to emphasise fine-grained frame-to-frame emotional change and has been combined with adaptive global attention for learner expression recognition in the literature \cite{liu_dynamic_nodate}. Full transformer stacks can capture rich cross-region and cross-time structure but typically demand more data and compute than lighter pipelines that pair a CNN--recurrent encoder with channel and temporal attention modules \cite{lek_academic_2023,su_leveraging_2024,aly_revolutionizing_2025}.

Several related terms appear alongside attention in the FER literature but serve different roles. \emph{MTCNN} is a cascaded face detector that locates and aligns face regions before expression classification; it is a preprocessing step rather than an attention mechanism \cite{tulsani_realtime_2025}. A \emph{Temporal Convolutional Network} (TCN) uses one-dimensional temporal convolutions instead of recurrent units to model dependencies over a frame sequence and can be used as an alternative temporal backbone in some DAiSEE-based designs \cite{abedi_improving_2021,aly_revolutionizing_2025}. The methodology chapter describes how channel and temporal attention are incorporated in the FER design used in this work.

\section{Multilabel Classification and Class Imbalance}

\emph{Multilabel classification} means that one input sample can activate more than one label at the same time \cite{lek_academic_2023}. In \emph{single-label} classification, by contrast, each sample is assigned to exactly one class, often through a softmax layer that forces the predicted probabilities to compete for a single winner \cite{gupta_daisee_2016}. On DAiSEE, the four academic states (engagement, boredom, confusion, and frustration) frequently co-occur in practice: a learner may appear engaged yet slightly confused, or bored with hints of frustration \cite{gupta_daisee_2016,dmello_language_2012}. Educational FER reviews therefore treat multilabel formulations as the standard match for DAiSEE-style annotations rather than collapsing clips to one dominant emotion \cite{lek_academic_2023,florestiyanto_emotion_2024}.

Similar academic states can nevertheless be difficult to separate from the face alone. Confusion and frustration, and sometimes boredom, often produce overlapping expressions and comparable behavioural cues, whereas basic-emotion benchmarks contrast more separable categories such as happy versus sad \cite{dmello_language_2012,lek_academic_2023}. Visually similar states are a recurring source of error in educational FER, which is why per-label decisions, per-label losses, and per-label evaluation are emphasised in the literature rather than a single winner-take-all label \cite{gupta_daisee_2016,florestiyanto_emotion_2024}. Some studies argue that additional modalities could aid disambiguation; unimodal facial pipelines must instead rely on temporal context, imbalance-aware training, and careful decision thresholds \cite{lek_academic_2023}.

For multilabel outputs, a \emph{sigmoid} activation is applied independently to each label logit, producing a value in $(0,1)$ that can be read as the probability that the state is active \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022,rianto_engagement_2025}. For label $l$, $p_l = \sigma(z_l) = 1/(1+\exp(-z_l))$. Because each label passes through its own sigmoid, the four decisions do not compete for a shared probability mass, unlike softmax multi-class heads \cite{rianto_engagement_2025,gupta_daisee_2016}. This independence matches the structure of multilabel academic affect on DAiSEE.

A \emph{loss function} measures prediction error during training \cite{abedi_improving_2021}. In multilabel FER, \emph{binary cross-entropy} with logits is standard: each label is treated as a separate binary problem \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022,rianto_engagement_2025}. \emph{Weighted} binary cross-entropy assigns a higher penalty to under-represented positive labels so the optimiser does not collapse toward predicting only the majority state; on DAiSEE training splits, engagement positives are far more frequent than confusion or frustration positives \cite{gupta_daisee_2016,mehta_three-dimensional_2022,santoni_convolutional_2023}. Alternative imbalance-aware objectives, such as large-margin or focal-style losses, appear in some recent studies but pursue the same goal of preventing minority labels from being ignored \cite{rianto_engagement_2025}.

Class imbalance is also addressed at the data level. \emph{Controlled oversampling} increases the frequency of minority clips in the training set according to fixed duplication rules, which changes what the model sees without altering the network architecture \cite{santoni_convolutional_2023,abedi_improving_2021}. \emph{SMOTE} (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) instead creates synthetic examples by interpolating between minority feature vectors and is used in some DAiSEE-based hybrid pipelines \cite{santoni_convolutional_2023,rao_recognition_2021}. Both strategies aim to reduce bias toward majority labels; the choice between planned duplication and synthetic oversampling remains a design decision in the literature.

After training, each label requires a \emph{decision threshold} to convert probabilities into active/inactive predictions. A default cutoff of $0.5$ is often suboptimal when class frequencies differ: it may favour recall on frequent labels and suppress rare ones \cite{rianto_engagement_2025,mehta_three-dimensional_2022}. \emph{Per-label threshold tuning} on a validation set, for example to improve per-label F1, is common in DAiSEE FER because harder labels such as confusion and frustration typically need different operating points than engagement \cite{rianto_engagement_2025,mehta_three-dimensional_2022,lek_academic_2023}. Threshold choice trades precision against recall separately for each emotion. The methodology chapter describes the multilabel formulation and imbalance handling adopted for DAiSEE in this work.

\section{Evaluation Metrics for Multilabel FER}\label{sec:bg:metrics}

On imbalanced multilabel DAiSEE data, a single accuracy figure is often misleading, especially when rare labels such as confusion or frustration are compared with frequent engagement \cite{gupta_daisee_2016,lek_academic_2023,mehta_three-dimensional_2022}. Educational FER studies therefore report \emph{precision} and \emph{recall} separately for each binary label, then summarise them with per-label \emph{F1} and aggregated variants \cite{rianto_engagement_2025,fu_integrating_2025}. \emph{Macro} F1 treats all four academic states equally and is widely used when fair comparison across rare and frequent labels matters; \emph{micro} and \emph{weighted} F1 instead reflect overall decision volume or label prevalence. Clip-level \emph{exact-match} accuracy and \emph{samples} F1 are stricter or clip-balanced summaries that complement per-label scores \cite{fu_integrating_2025,rianto_engagement_2025}. Some DAiSEE work still reports ordinal \emph{mean squared error} or single-label \emph{top-1 accuracy}; those formulations differ from multilabel binary evaluation on co-occurring states \cite{gupta_daisee_2016,su_leveraging_2024}. Formal definitions, thresholding rules, and the exact metric set used in this thesis are given in the methodology chapter; numerical results appear in the results chapter \cite{mehta_three-dimensional_2022,lek_academic_2023}.

\section{Adaptive Learning and Intelligent Tutoring Systems}\label{sec:bg:adaptive}

Adaptive learning systems are educational technologies that personalise instruction along several dimensions at once: content sequencing, pacing, difficulty, hints, scaffolding, worked examples, feedback, and motivational support, using data collected while the learner studies \cite{gligorea_adaptive_2023,strielkowski_span_2025,zerkouk_comprehensive_2025}. Educational accounts of effective tutoring emphasise flexibility and responsiveness rather than a single fixed path: a skilled human tutor notices affect, adjusts challenge, offers explanatory support, and acts early to limit disengagement \cite{pekrun_control-value_2006,yuvaraj_affective_2025}. The defining property of adaptive systems is a \emph{closed loop}: the environment observes behaviour and outcomes, updates an internal estimate of learner state, and selects the next pedagogical action accordingly \cite{gligorea_adaptive_2023,zerkouk_comprehensive_2025}. Continuous monitoring of performance and engagement during online study is widely treated as essential for timely personalisation \cite{strielkowski_span_2025,florestiyanto_emotion_2024}. Strong platforms also respond to longer learning trajectories, not only to the latest response \cite{fu_integrating_2025,guzman_developing_2025}. Conceptually, this places adaptive learning within \emph{sequential decision-making}, where each instructional choice has both immediate and longer-term consequences \cite{kabudi_ai-enabled_2021}. Reinforcement-learning-based tutors formalise this closed loop; many existing policies nevertheless optimise pacing or item sequencing from cognitive traces alone, without sensed affect. Algorithmic treatments of reinforcement learning for tutoring appear in the next section.

\subsection{Intelligent Tutoring Systems}\label{sec:bg:its}

An \emph{Intelligent Tutoring System} (ITS) is a computer-based learning environment that delivers individualised tutoring by reasoning over three components: a \emph{learner model} (what the student knows and how they behave), a \emph{domain model} (the subject matter and tasks), and a \emph{pedagogical model} (how to teach) \cite{zerkouk_comprehensive_2025,kabudi_ai-enabled_2021}. Classical ITS designs rely on hand-crafted rules to trigger hints, worked examples, remediation, or changes in difficulty, which limits optimisation of long-term learning trajectories across diverse learners \cite{guzman_developing_2025}. Recent work increasingly replaces fixed rule sets with data-driven policies that decide when to scaffold, explain, motivate, adjust pace, or schedule review, as well as which exercise to present next \cite{axak_adaptive_nodate,deshmukh_developing_2025,guzman_developing_2025,meng_self-evolving_2025}. Machine-learned policies are attractive when tutoring must balance many pedagogical levers over extended interactions. That shift connects ITS design to sequential decision-making frameworks in machine learning; the following section introduces reinforcement learning as one formalism for learning such policies, without prescribing a particular algorithm here.

\subsection{Knowledge Tracing and Deep Knowledge Tracing}\label{sec:bg:kt}

\emph{Knowledge tracing} (KT) is the task of estimating a learner's evolving mastery of skills or concepts from interaction data, typically sequences of exercises labelled with correctness, associated skill tags, and sometimes response times or help requests \cite{urbaite_adaptive_2026,kuling_ken_2025}. Accurate tracing supports adaptive instruction because the next pedagogical decision should depend on what the learner is ready to learn \cite{kuling_ken_2025,urbaite_adaptive_2026}. KT primarily models \emph{cognitive} progress; affective state is a separate signal unless an architecture explicitly integrates it, which remains uncommon in classical tutoring pipelines \cite{urbaite_adaptive_2026,axak_adaptive_nodate}.

\emph{Deep Knowledge Tracing} (DKT) denotes neural knowledge-tracing models in which recurrent or memory-augmented networks learn continuous mastery representations directly from interaction sequences \cite{urbaite_adaptive_2026,kuling_ken_2025,guzman_developing_2025}. Compared with earlier probabilistic tracers, DKT captures temporal structure among exercises more flexibly and is often paired with downstream components that adjust instructional pacing or content sequencing from estimated mastery \cite{fu_integrating_2025,guzman_developing_2025,meng_self-evolving_2025}. Conceptually, KT and DKT \emph{predict} latent knowledge; a separate policy module may then \emph{choose} pedagogical actions such as hints, pacing changes, or problem selection. Recent systems can combine deep tracing with reinforcement-learning recommenders, but the two roles remain distinct: tracing estimates competence, while the tutor policy commits to instructional interventions \cite{fu_integrating_2025,guzman_developing_2025}. The methodology chapter describes how adaptive instruction and knowledge progress are represented in the tutoring environment used in this work.

\section{Reinforcement Learning}\label{sec:bg:rl}

The closed-loop, sequential nature of adaptive instruction motivates reinforcement learning (RL) as a unifying decision-making framework. RL studies how a policy can be learned from interaction with an environment so as to maximise expected long-term reward \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. The standard formalism is a Markov Decision Process (MDP), written $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space, $\mathcal{A}$ the action space, $P$ the transition dynamics, $R$ the reward function, and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ a discount factor \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. At each step the environment presents a state $s_t \in \mathcal{S}$; the policy selects an action $a_t \in \mathcal{A}$; the learner receives reward $r_t$ and transitions to $s_{t+1}$. The objective is a policy that maximises the expected discounted return $\mathbb{E}[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t]$ \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}.

This mapping is natural for tutoring: the state summarises cognitive progress and, in affect-aware systems, perceived academic emotions such as engagement or frustration; the action is a \emph{discrete} pedagogical intervention drawn from a finite set, for example hints, scaffolding, pacing changes, worked examples, explanatory strategies, difficulty adjustment, or motivational support \cite{axak_adaptive_nodate,perez_emotions_2024,gong_design_2025,deshmukh_developing_2025}. The reward reflects both learning progress and, in holistic designs, affective well-being \cite{perez_emotions_2024,gong_design_2025,fu_integrating_2025}. Much educational RL has emphasised cognitive traces such as correctness or mastery while paying less attention to frustration, confusion, and boredom, even though these states influence persistence and performance \cite{axak_adaptive_nodate,meng_self-evolving_2025,urbaite_adaptive_2026}. Deep reinforcement learning has also been proposed for real-time personalisation from joint performance and emotional cues \cite{govea_implementation_2024,gong_design_2025}. Some adaptive controllers apply policy optimisation to pacing or sequencing without explicit affect in the state \cite{axak_adaptive_nodate}; affect-aware extensions feed perceived emotion into the state and sometimes into reward design \cite{perez_emotions_2024,gong_design_2025}. The methodology chapter specifies the Markov decision process, reward formulation, and algorithms used in the adaptation module.

\subsection{Model-Based and Model-Free Reinforcement Learning}\label{sec:bg:model_free}

Reinforcement-learning methods are often divided into \emph{model-based} and \emph{model-free} families \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. Model-based approaches, such as Dyna-Q, learn or use an explicit model of environment transitions to simulate experience and plan ahead. Model-free approaches, including Q-learning, SARSA, DQN, and PPO, update value functions or policies directly from sampled transitions without maintaining a full environment model \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018,mnih_dqn_2015,guzman_developing_2025}. In unpredictable classrooms and tutoring simulations, where affect can change quickly and learner responses are noisy, building an accurate, timely transition model is difficult; model-free deep RL is therefore common in educational tutoring research \cite{axak_adaptive_nodate,azoulay_adaptive_2021,guzman_developing_2025}. Tabular model-free value methods suit small discrete action spaces but struggle in high-dimensional state spaces without function approximation, which motivates deep Q-networks and policy-gradient algorithms \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018,mnih_dqn_2015}. The adaptation module in this work belongs to the model-free family.

These methods must balance \emph{exploration} (trying actions to discover better long-term strategies) and \emph{exploitation} (using the current best policy) \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. In educational settings, affective feedback is variable and measurement noise is high, so early exploratory policies may recommend weak interventions, while excessive exploitation can lock onto suboptimal tutoring rules. That tension is one reason why policies are often trained in simulators before deployment, as discussed in the following section on simulation-based evaluation.

\subsection{Value-Based Methods and Q-Learning}\label{sec:bg:qlearning}

A central object in value-based RL is the action-value function $Q^{\pi}(s,a)$: the expected discounted return from taking action $a$ in state $s$ and following policy $\pi$ thereafter \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. The optimal $Q^{\star}$ satisfies the Bellman optimality relation, and a greedy policy with respect to $Q^{\star}$ is optimal in finite MDPs \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. \emph{Q-learning} is an off-policy temporal-difference algorithm that estimates $Q^{\star}$ from transitions without a model of the environment. A tabular update takes the form
\begin{equation}
  Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha \Bigl[ r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a) \Bigr],
  \label{eq:q-learning}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a learning rate \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. Q-learning is well suited to \emph{discrete} action spaces when states are few; for richer tutor states it is extended through function approximators, notably deep Q-networks \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018,mnih_dqn_2015}.

\subsection{Deep Q-Networks}\label{sec:bg:dqn}

The Deep Q-Network (DQN) represents $Q(s,a)$ with a deep neural network \cite{mnih_dqn_2015}. Two stabilising mechanisms are standard: an \emph{experience replay} buffer, which stores past transitions and samples mini-batches to decorrelate updates, and a periodically updated \emph{target network} for bootstrap targets \cite{mnih_dqn_2015}. DQN is a natural fit for tutoring MDPs with finite pedagogical action sets, because the agent selects among discrete interventions such as offering a hint, changing pace, or adjusting problem difficulty \cite{axak_adaptive_nodate,kong_real-time_2025,gong_design_2025,perez_emotions_2024,deshmukh_developing_2025}. Educational studies have used DQN-style agents in simulated and platform-based tutors when states combine performance traces with affective features \cite{govea_implementation_2024,sayed_ai-based_2023}.

\subsection{Policy Optimisation: Proximal Policy Optimisation}\label{sec:bg:ppo}

\emph{Proximal Policy Optimisation} (PPO) is a model-free policy-gradient method known for stable updates: a clipped surrogate objective limits how much the policy can change between optimisation steps, which reduces collapse when rewards are noisy \cite{guzman_developing_2025,axak_adaptive_nodate}. PPO is widely used in adaptive tutoring and Gym-style learning environments alongside value-based methods \cite{guzman_developing_2025,govea_implementation_2024,axak_adaptive_nodate}. Although PPO is often illustrated with continuous control in robotics, tutoring MDPs typically use \emph{finite discrete} action spaces; the policy then outputs a categorical distribution over pedagogical actions such as hint types or pacing modes \cite{guzman_developing_2025,deshmukh_developing_2025}. PPO does not require continuous actions, and it should not be paired conceptually only with long-horizon curriculum control while DQN handles short horizons: both families can support multi-step tutoring with different stability and sample-efficiency trade-offs \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018,guzman_developing_2025}.

Compared with Q-learning and DQN, PPO optimises the policy directly rather than through a separate value-maximisation step, which can stabilise learning when rewards mix cognitive and affective terms \cite{guzman_developing_2025,perez_emotions_2024}. DQN remains strong when discrete Q-values with replay are sufficient; PPO is attractive when stable policy gradients over stochastic tutoring policies are needed. Affect-aware tutoring research has reported both approaches; comparative evaluation depends on state design, reward structure, and simulator fidelity rather than on a single universally superior algorithm \cite{govea_implementation_2024,axak_adaptive_nodate,guzman_developing_2025,deshmukh_developing_2025}.

\section{Simulation-Based Evaluation in RL Tutoring Systems}\label{sec:bg:simulation}

Reinforcement learning is fundamentally a trial-and-error procedure: a useful tutoring policy typically requires many state–action trials before stable behaviour emerges \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:bg:model_free}, early policies are under-trained and may recommend weak or disruptive interventions if deployed immediately on learners \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018}. Carrying out large-scale exploration directly on human subjects is therefore often impractical for ethical and pedagogical reasons, as well as because RL sample requirements are high and online policy updates can harm the learning experience \cite{azoulay_adaptive_2021,axak_adaptive_nodate}. Training and testing on simulated student profiles derived from interaction data is a common pattern before held-out or live evaluation \cite{azoulay_adaptive_2021,deshmukh_developing_2025,weitekamp_tutorgym_2025}.

A \emph{learner simulator} is an executable model that, given a tutor action and a representation of the current learner state, returns a next state and a scalar reward \cite{azoulay_adaptive_2021,weitekamp_tutorgym_2025,axak_adaptive_nodate}. Simulators may encode \emph{cognitive} variables such as mastery or answer correctness, \emph{behavioural} variables such as help-seeking or response timing, and \emph{affective} variables such as engagement, confusion, or frustration \cite{axak_adaptive_nodate,perez_emotions_2024}. Recent ITS reinforcement-learning work often exposes such environments through standard reinforcement-learning interfaces, for example OpenAI Gym-style step and reset calls, so that rule-based, bandit, and deep RL agents can be swapped under the same transition dynamics \cite{weitekamp_tutorgym_2025,axak_adaptive_nodate}.

Educational affect research also distinguishes \emph{static} perception benchmarks from \emph{dynamic} tutoring evaluation. Curated video datasets and predefined recording scenarios are valuable for training and benchmarking facial expression recognition, but they do not fully capture how academic affect fluctuates during continuous online study \cite{gupta_daisee_2016,lek_academic_2023}. Personalisation at tutoring time requires systems that can update estimates of performance and affect as interaction unfolds, not only classify offline clips. A common two-stage pattern in the literature is therefore to develop or validate perception on benchmark video data, then embed perceived affect and performance signals in a step-wise simulator that refreshes state at each pedagogical decision when training or comparing RL policies \cite{perez_emotions_2024,gong_design_2025,lek_academic_2023}.

Within that pattern, simulators serve several recurring roles in RL tutoring research. They make policy learning \emph{feasible} by providing a safe, repeatable source of large numbers of interactions \cite{azoulay_adaptive_2021,deshmukh_developing_2025}. They support \emph{controlled comparison}, because rule-based, random, bandit-style, and RL policies can be evaluated on the same simulated cohort or matched seeds, isolating algorithmic differences \cite{azoulay_adaptive_2021,axak_adaptive_nodate,weitekamp_tutorgym_2025}. They also enable \emph{ablation studies}, such as contrasting state representations that include sensed affect with matched emotion-agnostic baselines under identical simulation conditions \cite{weitekamp_tutorgym_2025,perez_emotions_2024}. These uses inform how simulation findings should be read: as evidence about policy behaviour under stated assumptions, not as guaranteed classroom learning gains.

Simulation does not remove all deployment risks. RL-based tutors face a \emph{cold-start} problem: until enough interaction data have been observed, policies may be unreliable, and offline or simulator training only partially mitigates that gap on real learners \cite{sutton_rl_book_2018,axak_adaptive_nodate}. There is also a \emph{sim-to-real} gap: policies optimised in simulation may transfer poorly to classrooms because real learners are more heterogeneous, non-stationary, and only partially observable \cite{urbaite_adaptive_2026,azoulay_adaptive_2021}. The methodology and results chapters describe the simulator and evaluation protocol used in this work.
